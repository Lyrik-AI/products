## What is the tuk-tuk food ride?

It's a ride into the old workers' quarter tucked behind Chengdu's east gate: an hour and a half to two hours, four or five family-run shops, four or five local dishes you shouldn't leave without, and a food guide sitting beside you the whole way, eating and talking. The narrow lanes keep the coaches out, but that's the least of why this works. The route was drawn to take you deep into how one old working quarter really lives and eats: the cooks here answer to their neighbors, not to visitors, and each stop was chosen for what it opens up, not for what a bus can reach. You won't share a table with another group, and no packaged tour is built to go this far in.

## What you'll taste and do

A tuk-tuk through car-free lanes

Ride low and open down streets a coach can't turn into, the old east-side quarter passing at elbow height, seven or eight minutes of open air between each stop.

A flexible selection of more than ten local dishes

No wandering a food street guessing which stall is any good; your guide orders the right thing at each stop and hands it over ready to eat.

A guide who stays, not a guide who points

Someone rides the whole route with you, reads each dish back to its street and its history, and turns the heat up or down to match the table.

Tailored to Your Preferences

Vegetarian, no pork, no beef, less chili: the route bends to how you eat, worked out before you climb aboard.

## How the ride runs

Start · Meet-up

Meet your guide at street level and climb onto the tuk-tuk. The meeting point is sent to you after booking.

Stop 01 · Guokui & fei chang fen

A shop past its twenty-fifth year on an old east-gate street: a griddle-fired flatbread and a bowl of sweet-potato noodles in pig-intestine broth: the carb-heavy breakfast Chengdu's workers have leaned on for generations. Guests from Hong Kong and Taiwan often clock it the moment they sit down: this is the shop from Nicholas Tse's Chengdu episode of Chef Nic. We don't print the name; you'll know when you're there.

![Crisp guokui over a bowl of fei chang fen](https://cdn.lyriktrip.com/s/47bfa9a83f31.jpg)

Stop 02 · Dan hong gao

A folded egg pancake first sold here in the late Qing, more than a hundred years back, still griddled to order in copper pans by the Nie family, who started from a pushcart and built a shop on taste alone. Don't be fooled by the thin shell and the modest filling: it carries more than it looks. Sweet or savory, handed over warm.

![Dan hong gao cooking in copper griddle pans over coals](https://cdn.lyriktrip.com/s/7f5679845cc8.jpg)

Stop 03 · Chao shou

The real centerpiece. The chao shou, Chengdu's thin-skinned wonton under fragrant-then-hot red oil, is only half of it. This block is where the lanes of our name actually come from: 1960s tenement walk-ups where old Chengdu still lives, where you'll hear no tourist Mandarin and no English, and where no coach can turn in. Over a hot bowl, the owner tells you what this street has lived through.

![Chao shou: Chengdu's wonton under red oil](https://cdn.lyriktrip.com/s/f3037f9ee594.jpg)

Stop 04 · Bo bo ji

Cold skewers you lift yourself from a pot of chili-and-Sichuan-pepper broth, numbing and bright, eaten standing, a small sweet bowl of tian shui mian, sweet-water noodles, alongside to reset your palate between skewers; not the lead, just the relief.

![A table of bo bo ji skewers and sides](https://cdn.lyriktrip.com/s/64f957e7d484.jpg)

Near Jiuyan Bridge · The rest is yours

The ride ends at the river's bar street: stay out for a drink, or point the taxi home.

## The local drivers behind the journey

The tuk-tuks are driven by members of a local drivers' community, including drivers with mild physical disabilities.

These compact vehicles allow them to keep working independently, taking guests into neighborhood lanes that larger vehicles cannot reach.

Their knowledge of the streets is not an added performance. It is part of the way the ride naturally unfolds.

![A tuk-tuk driver at the window of his blue cab](https://cdn.lyriktrip.com/s/d25258518708.jpg)

> "We ran this route dozens of times before it settled: which stop comes first, how much to say at each, when to let you just eat. Give a stranger the name of these shops and they still won't find them: no English signs, no reviews, no bus that stops nearby. That's the point."

Charles Yang · LyrikTrip Travel Designer, Chengdu

## FAQ

Will there be organ meat or anything unusual?

One plate leans adventurous: the pig intestine in the first bowl. It arrives cooked and plated, never raw at the table, and we'll flag or swap it if you'd rather skip it.

Are these hole-in-the-wall places clean?

This is a meal among neighborhood cooks, so the rooms are plain. Your guide reaches each stop first, checks the table and setting, and has it put right before you sit down.

Can you cook around my diet or lower the spice?

Yes. Tell us in advance for vegetarian, no pork, or no beef, and the route bends to it. Spice comes down to mild on request. One honest note: a fully vegetarian table has fewer options here: count on about two of the five dishes.

I don't have WeChat. How do we meet?

No problem. We confirm the meeting point with you after booking, no Chinese number or Chinese apps needed.

How much walking is there, and can children come?

Families are welcome: the tuk-tuk carries you between stops and the pace stays gentle, so children ride easily. The walks are short but run over uneven old-lane pavement and low stools; tell us about any mobility needs and we'll advise before you travel.
