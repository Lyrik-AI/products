## Two clocks

Neiliansheng opened in 1853, making court boots for the palace and for officials — nobody else. The name says so: nei is the palace interior, and liansheng is the promise that whoever wears them gets promoted three ranks. Then the dynasty fell, the technique went into ordinary shoes — and this morning it sits next to a pot of tea that takes four minutes.

![The Neiliansheng shopfront in Beijing](https://cdn.lyriktrip.com/s/3b47a40396b1.webp)

## Two crafts, and what each one leaves you with

Half a day at two deliberately opposite speeds: a shoe sole that takes four or five days and over ninety separate hand operations, then a tea table where the entire skill is refusing to hurry.

You'll see

- A sole being stitched through dozens of pasted cotton layers with waxed hemp
- The wooden last, and the seam that has to stay even the whole way round
- The full range — fifteen hundred styles have come out of this technique since 1853
- A tea table laid properly, everything within one arm's reach

You'll drink

- The first infusion — which gets thrown away, and you'll learn why
- The second, which is the one worth having
- The same leaf at three different steeping times, side by side

You'll hear

- Why the shop is called Neiliansheng — nei is the palace, liansheng is a promotion of three ranks
- That an apprentice needs three years before touching the final seam
- Why nobody has found a faster way that still works

You'll take home

- The pouring sequence, which you can do at any table afterwards
- A reason to look at the bottom of a shoe
- A calibrated sense of what 'handmade' should actually cost

## How it unfolds

09:20 · A thousand layers, ninety operations

The craft describes itself as one high and four many: high standards, and many operations, many stitching patterns, many ways of joining upper to sole, many styles of shoe. Cotton scraps are pasted into a slab, dried, then stitched through with waxed hemp — every stitch pulled tight enough to compress the stack. Four or five days per pair, and no shortcut has ever stuck.

![The white layered sole of a cloth shoe, seen from the side](https://cdn.lyriktrip.com/s/285802852c5d.webp)

10:10 · Three years before you touch it

The upper is cut, shaped over a wooden last, and joined to the sole with a seam that must stay even the whole way round. This is the step an apprentice reaches after three years, and the reason the technique is listed as at risk: it takes too long to learn and fewer people start. You watch it; you do not attempt it.

![Hand stitching and appliqué on an antique cloth shoe](https://cdn.lyriktrip.com/s/6a510f380591.webp)

11:20 · Now nothing is urgent

Warm the vessel, rinse the leaf, and pour — then pour again, because the second infusion is usually the one worth drinking. You learn the sequence by doing it: how full, how long, when to stop. A short list of rules that takes a lifetime to stop thinking about.

![The hall of Laoshe Teahouse, hung with red lanterns](https://cdn.lyriktrip.com/s/bdc370040376.webp)

11:50 · You pour for the table

Cups are never filled one at a time. You go round the table in passes so everyone's cup ends up the same strength — which is the actual point of the ceremony and the reason it looks fussy from outside. Do it once and it stops looking fussy.

![Wooden chairs and tables set out in the teahouse hall](https://cdn.lyriktrip.com/s/c3fe48caea7e.webp)

## One takes ninety operations. The other takes four minutes.

Not an accident of scheduling

Beijing is full of both and rarely shows you them together. A sole is days of repetitive, unglamorous work that nobody watches. A pot of tea is over almost as soon as it starts, and everybody watches.

What they share is that both are entirely about restraint — even stitches, even pours — and both are ruined by rushing in exactly the same way.

Neiliansheng has produced more than fifteen hundred styles since 1853. Not one of them got faster.

![Another antique pair, the cloth sole visible under the embroidery](https://cdn.lyriktrip.com/s/04c7811a9444.webp)
