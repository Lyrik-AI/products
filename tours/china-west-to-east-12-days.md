### Day 2 · After the pandas, nothing else matters

*The morning does the thing everyone at home asked about. The afternoon does the thing nobody warned you about.*

**Chengdu panda base**

you are there in the morning, the hours the animals are generally moving: eating, climbing down off a platform, falling asleep again in the fork of a tree. Flat shaded paths and about three hours. Get to an enclosure early and wait rather than walk on: the animal comes to the fence on its own schedule, not on yours.

**People's Park teahouse**

bamboo chairs under the trees, a gaiwan each, the lidded cup China brews in, and a man with a long-spouted kettle who tops you up for as long as you keep the seat. At the next table, cards; two tables over, an ear cleaner working with a tuning fork. Half an hour in, you'll notice you have stopped planning the rest of the day.

**Pace**

this is the light day of the twelve, on purpose, and it is placed on your second day, when your body clock is still wrong.

### Day 3 · A machine that is still on duty

*Out of the city by high-speed rail and back the same evening, bags left in your room. Today is where the trip's clock starts running.*

**Dujiangyan irrigation works**

you walk the levee between two flows of the same river, one sent to the fields and one left to the flood. Work began in the third century BC and the system is still doing the job. Your guide shows you the fish-mouth divide, then the spillway, then the neck cut through the rock, in that order, because the order is the argument. Standing on the divide with the water going both ways, you'll find the word "ancient" has stopped fitting.

**Mount Qingcheng Taoist temple**

a quiet working temple rather than the crowded halls by the front gate. You sit down with a resident Taoist, your guide interpreting, and then take a first tai chi lesson in the courtyard: weight into one leg, then the other, slower than feels natural.

**Back to Chengdu**

the evening train back, and the same hotel room. Three nights in Chengdu means the bags are opened once.

<sub>🛏️ **Stay:** Chengdu</sub>

<sub>🍽️ **Meals:** Breakfast at the hotel</sub>

### Day 4 · Lanes, a painted face, then the mountains

*A last Chengdu morning at street level, and an afternoon train that changes the climate outside the window.*

**Kuanzhai Alley and Taikoo Li**

three parallel lanes of restored courtyard frontage, busy and commercial, which is what they are; a short walk away, Taikoo Li puts the same city's present tense next to it. Stand where the two meet and take the photograph: it is the whole trip's argument in one frame.

**Community Sichuan opera**

a neighborhood house, backstage before the doors open, and then face-changing from three rows back. The mask goes from one painted face to another in the time it takes the performer to turn his head, and you will not catch it. Watch his left hand, not his face; you still will not catch it.

**Rail to Xi'an**

the afternoon train runs north through the Qinling range, the mountain wall that divides the south of China from the north. Tunnel, then valley, then tunnel; when the window clears for good, the green has gone out of the landscape and you are in the dry country the empire started in.

<sub>🛏️ **Stay:** Xi'an</sub>

<sub>🍽️ **Meals:** Breakfast at the hotel</sub>

### Day 5 · Eight thousand faces

*One site, one day, no filler around it. The point is not that they are old; the point is that they are individuals.*

**The Terracotta Warriors**

your guide teaches you to read them before you go in: armor for rank, hair knots for role, the general standing where the general stands, crossbowmen at the front. Then you go up to the rail of the main pit and the estimated eight thousand of them are standing in formation below you, in the ground where they were found rather than in cases.

**Newer excavation sites**

in the more recently worked areas, traces of the original paint are still on the figures. They were not gray. Stand close to the rail on the excavation side and look for the red and the green in the folds.

**Pace**

much of this day is spent standing, so the site is worked in two halves with a sit-down between them; the vehicle stays with you.

<sub>🛏️ **Stay:** Xi'an</sub>

<sub>🍽️ **Meals:** Breakfast at the hotel</sub>

### Day 6 · Above the traffic, then into the lanes

*An hour of the trip where you choose the direction, and an evening where you choose the dinner.*

**The Xi'an City Wall by bicycle**

up the ramp onto the top of the wall, where the surface is wide enough for two bicycles side by side, and out along the circuit with your guide. Old city on your right, new city on your left, both of them below you. Ten minutes along the parapet, you'll find yourself reading the city as a plan instead of a maze.

**Bell and Drum Tower quarter**

the two towers that kept the city's time before clocks, standing in the middle of the traffic that now goes around them. Your guide takes the quarter on foot at dusk, when the lights on both towers come up.

**The Muslim Quarter at night**

lanes the city's Hui Muslim community has cooked and traded in for centuries: hand-pulled noodles thrown against the bench, whole spices in open sacks, lamb going onto skewers by the hundred. You buy stall by stall, on your own account, and your guide tells you which stall is worth the queue.

<sub>🍽️ **Meals:** Breakfast at the hotel</sub>

in through the gates and along one line: the ceremonial halls where the court did its business, the living quarters behind them, the imperial garden at the end. Finished in 1420, lived in for five centuries. Stand in the middle of the main courtyard and look back at the gates you came through: they line up one inside another, all the way out to the street.

### Day 9 · The Great Wall, No Climbing Required

three structures on one line, where the emperor came once a year to account for the harvest. Round for heaven, square for earth, and the same grammar repeated at every scale from the altar to the railings. two hours on the east side of the square, and the objects behind the last nine days end up in one building: bronzes, ceramics, Buddhist figures, calligraphy. It is placed here on purpose, as the summary before you leave the north.

two hours with the bronzes, the ceramics and the painting. Pick one material and follow it through the halls: the same country you have been walking for ten days, told through one substance at a time.

**The Bund after dark**

the stone banking houses on one bank, the towers of Pudong on the other, a hundred years apart and looking straight at each other across the water. Your guide is with you all evening.

## China Ready

We've solved the hard parts. Here's everything a first-time Western traveler needs to know before arriving.

### 🔐 VPN & Internet Access

<sub>Do this before you fly</sub>

- **What's blocked:** Google, Gmail, YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, WhatsApp, and most Western news sites are inaccessible in China without a VPN.
- **Download before you leave:** It's nearly impossible to download a VPN once inside China. Install and test at home. Recommended: Astrill (most reliable), Mullvad, ExpressVPN — subscribe to at least two in case one stops working.
- **Hotel Wi-Fi tip:** Some hotels block VPN connections on their Wi-Fi. Use your phone's mobile data as a hotspot if the VPN drops.
- **Good news:** WhatsApp works perfectly through a VPN, so your messaging stays unchanged. Apple iMessage and Signal also work.

### 💳 Paying in China

<sub>Your concierge sets this up on Day 1</sub>

- **China is cashless:** Alipay and WeChat Pay are used for almost everything — restaurants, taxis, markets, tickets. Cash is rarely accepted in cities.
- **International cards now work:** Foreign Visa/Mastercard can now link directly to Alipay (set up in minutes, \$5,000 single transaction limit). Your concierge walks you through the setup on your first morning.
- **Carry some cash:** We recommend ¥500–800 RMB in cash for the rare vendor that won't accept digital payment, and for small towns.
- **Booking this trip:** Your deposit and balance are charged via Stripe — the same trusted platform used by Airbnb and Booking.com. USD billing, no surprise conversion fees.

### 🛂 Visa & Entry

<sub>We handle the paperwork</sub>

- **Do you need a visa?:** Most US, UK, EU, Australian, Canadian, and New Zealand passport holders need a tourist (L) visa for stays over 10 days. We provide a visa invitation letter with every booking — it significantly speeds up approval.
- **240-hour visa-free transit:** Citizens of 55+ countries (including USA, UK, most of EU, AUS, NZ, Canada) can enter China visa-free for up to 10 days if transiting through eligible ports. This covers our 12-day itinerary only if you have a confirmed onward flight.
- **Processing time:** Apply 4–6 weeks before departure. Standard processing: 4–7 business days. Express (3 days) and same-day options available at most Chinese consulates.
- **What we provide:** Visa invitation letter, hotel confirmation for all nights, suggested itinerary document — everything the consulate needs, formatted correctly.

### 🛡️ Safety & Practical Tips

<sub>Our responsibility, always</sub>

- **Is China safe?:** China consistently ranks among the safest countries for foreign tourists. Violent crime against visitors is extremely rare. Cities have extensive CCTV coverage and a visible police presence.
- **Emergency numbers:** Police: 110. Ambulance: 120. Your concierge is your first call for any non-emergency situation — she has local contacts for every city on the itinerary.
- **Health & medications:** Bring a full supply of any prescription medication. International pharmacies exist in major cities but may not carry your brand. Travel insurance is required — we can recommend policies that cover China specifically.

## Before you book — Upstream to the Sea FAQ

### Why start in Chengdu instead of Beijing?

Because the country then arrives in the order it happened, and because Chengdu is a gentler place to land. Day three puts you on a waterworks from the third century BC; day five on the first emperor's army; day eight in the palace two later dynasties ran; day eleven on the Bund. The cost of this choice is honest: Chengdu has thinner direct-flight coverage than Beijing, and some guests reach it on a connection. ### Four train rides in twelve days. Is that a lot of time on the move?

Less than four flights would be. All four are high-speed rail, city center to city center, and none of them needs an airport. Two are half days, Chengdu to Xi'an and Xi'an to Beijing; the Beijing to Shanghai run is an afternoon; the Dujiangyan pair on day three are short hops out and back with your bags left in the hotel.
