## Overview

Most routes across China are built from cities; this one is built from stone. In Zhangjiajie, you stand above sandstone pillars weathered into towers. In Yangshuo, you drift between limestone karsts, water having dissolved them into the very same shapes from within. In Shenzhen, the final block of rock is cast in concrete, its skyline rising in the span of a single working generation. Shanghai, where all currents meet, opens the journey. A full day is set aside for flight alone, while the middle of the route lingers long enough that the days stop being counted. By the second week, you will find yourself reading skylines the way you read cliffs.

*Stone stacked by hand in the morning, stone cast into towers at night. Today sets the question the next two weeks answer.*

**Yu Garden and the old city**

pavilions, ponds and a path that never runs straight, dropped inside the oldest quarter of the city. Stand at the rockery and look at how it was built: hauled stone, stacked by hand, made to read as a mountain range from six feet away.

**Shanghai Museum**

**The Bund after dark**

the historic bank buildings hold one side of the river and the Pudong towers hold the other. Your guide stays with you from start to finish tonight, which is deliberate: the first evening is when strangers approach visitors here with an invitation to practice English or to a teahouse, and that approach is a known scam pattern.

### Day 3 · Paper and water

*Streets at street level, then thirty minutes on a commuter boat. Today is Shanghai without a skyline in it.*

**Former French Concession and Wukang Road**

plane trees over both sides of the road, historic residences behind low walls, small shops at ground level. Walk the corner where the road forks and look up at the building on the point: the whole neighborhood is built on that angle.

**Woodblock watermark printing**

an hour and a half at a bench with carved blocks, water-based ink and absorbent paper. The ink is thinned and the paper takes it in rather than holding it on the surface, which is why the finished print looks brushed rather than stamped.

**Huangpu River ferry**

you cross on the boat people take to work, standing at the rail with commuters and their scooters. Thirty minutes door to door, and the two banks trade places around you.

<sub>🛏️ **Stay:** Shanghai</sub>

<sub>🍽️ **Meals:** Hotel breakfast is subject to written quote</sub>

## China Ready

### 🔐 VPN & Internet Access

<sub>Do this before you fly</sub>

- **What's blocked:** Google, Gmail, YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, WhatsApp, and most Western news sites are inaccessible in China without a VPN.
- **Download before you leave:** It's nearly impossible to download a VPN once inside China. Install and test at home. Recommended: Astrill (most reliable), Mullvad, ExpressVPN — subscribe to at least two in case one stops working.
- **Hotel Wi-Fi tip:** Some hotels block VPN connections on their Wi-Fi. Use your phone's mobile data as a hotspot if the VPN drops.
- **Good news:** WhatsApp works perfectly through a VPN, so your messaging stays unchanged. Apple iMessage and Signal also work.

### 💳 Paying in China

<sub>Your concierge sets this up on Day 1</sub>

- **China is cashless:** Alipay and WeChat Pay are used for almost everything — restaurants, taxis, markets, tickets. Cash is rarely accepted in cities.
- **International cards now work:** Foreign Visa/Mastercard can now link directly to Alipay (set up in minutes, \$5,000 single transaction limit). Your concierge walks you through the setup on your first morning.
- **Carry some cash:** We recommend ¥500–800 RMB in cash for the rare vendor that won't accept digital payment, and for small towns.

## Before you book — What the Stone Becomes FAQ

### Fifteen days and no river cruise. What holds this together?

One material, in three states. Zhangjiajie is sandstone weathered into towers you stand on top of; Yangshuo is limestone the river is dissolving from the inside, which you see from the water on day 10 and from underground on day 12; Shenzhen is the poured version, a skyline that went up within a working lifetime. Shanghai opens at the water where all of it drains to. The cruise was left out on purpose: three nights on a boat would have filled the calendar and taken the trip off its subject.

### I don't like heights. Is the glass bridge a problem?

No, and you do not have to negotiate it with anyone. A solid floor path runs beside the glass section for its whole length, so you can cross on solid ground from end to end. It costs nothing extra and takes nothing off your price. The Bailong Elevator on day 5 has glass on the valley side too, and it takes about two minutes; if that is also not for you, tell your guide and the walk-up route is arranged instead.

### We're looking at December. Can we book?

Not yet, and here is why. In December the Zhangjiajie installations stop for high wind, ice or annual maintenance. That is the cableways, the outdoor elevator, the cliff walkways and the bridge. The Li River enters low water at the same time, so the Xingping raft is often out of service. We hold no operating evidence for the coming season, so we do not open December for sale and we take no deposit for it. What you can do now: give us your dates and we will confirm the seasonal calendar with our ground partners and write to you with a straight answer, either a December departure that works or the nearest October or November dates, which are not held by this rule.

### How much walking is in this?

Four of the fifteen days carry real effort: two on the Zhangjiajie clifftops, the Yulong River day, and the pre-dawn steps at Xianggong Hill. Everything else is paced under that. The climb to the pillar tops is done by an outdoor lift rather than on foot. The Yulong River section has three modes chosen on the morning: bicycle, electric bike or vehicle, and taking the vehicle keeps you with the group. The Golden Whip Stream walk is cut to the group's pace anywhere from about 60 to 180 minutes. The Xianggong Hill sunrise is stone steps in the dark, and guests who stay in are not charged for it.

### Do we need a visa?

Most guests on this route do not. Most European passports enter visa-free for stays days; UK, Canadian, Australian and New Zealand ones the same. We check your 15 days plus any extra days in China against that window before you pay. The current policy runs through December 31, 2026, so departures on or before December 17, 2026 finish inside it. US passports are a different matter: this route is not sold to US passport holders at all, because 15 days runs past the ten-day visa-free transit stay window and Shenzhen has no direct service to the United States. We say that before any payment rather than working around it.
